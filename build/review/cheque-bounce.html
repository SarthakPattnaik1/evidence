A cheque bounced — I gave it or I received it

Please check every statement below against the current law and practice. Write corrections in the last column. Saboot is a free preparation tool: it lists papers and facts to take to an advocate and never recommends an advocate.

1. What the law says (shown to users)

Cheque dishonour is a criminal complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act. Deadlines run from specific events, so exact dates matter. Since 1 November 2025 (Supreme Court, Sanjabij Tari), summons can be served by email and WhatsApp, the accused can pay online at the start, and settlement costs were reduced: no cost if paid before the accused's evidence, 5% before the trial court's judgment, 7.5% in the High Court and 10% in the Supreme Court.

  • Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881
  • Sanjabij Tari v. Kishore S. Borcar, Supreme Court, 25 Sep 2025 — 2025 INSC 1158
  • Limitation Act, 1963

2. Questions asked

QuestionAnswers offeredCorrection
Which side are you on?
Why: The person who received the cheque and the person who issued it need completely different records.		I received the cheque and it bounced; I issued the cheque and it bounced; I issued it and got a legal notice or court summons
What reason is written on the bank's return memo?
Why: "Funds insufficient" and "exceeds arrangement" usually support a case. Signature or account-closed reasons need a closer look.		Funds insufficient / exceeds arrangement; Payment stopped by drawer; Account closed; Signature differs / other technical reason; No memo yet; I'm not sure
When did the bank tell you the cheque bounced? (asked only in some cases)
Why: A written demand notice must usually be sent within 30 days of learning about the dishonour.		Within the last 15 days; 15 to 30 days ago; More than 30 days ago; I'm not sure
Have you sent a written demand notice to the person who issued it? (asked only in some cases)
Why: Without a notice sent in time, a cheque-bounce complaint usually cannot proceed.		Yes; Not yet
How long ago was the notice delivered? (asked only in some cases)
Why: After delivery the issuer gets 15 days to pay. The complaint is then due within one month.		Less than 15 days ago; 15 to 45 days ago; More than 45 days ago; It came back undelivered or refused; I'm not sure
Why was the cheque given?
Why: The law presumes a cheque was for a debt, but the issuer can try to disprove this. The reason matters for both sides.		Repayment of a loan; Payment for goods or services; As "security" for a loan or deal; Signed blank cheque given earlier; Something else
Roughly how much is the cheque for?
Why: The amount affects fees and the interim compensation a court can order.		Up to ₹50,000; ₹50,000 to ₹5 lakh; More than ₹5 lakh
What have you received? (asked only in some cases)
Why: A notice gives you a chance to pay within 15 days. A court summons means a case is already filed.		A legal (demand) notice; A court summons; A warrant; I'm not sure
Do you dispute that you owed this amount? (asked only in some cases)
Why: If the money was already paid, not owed, or the cheque was misused, records proving that become central.		No, I owe it; I have already paid part or all of it; I never owed this amount; The cheque was given as security or misused
Were other cheques or payments part of the same deal? (detailed round)
Why: Several cheques can mean several complaints, and earlier payments affect the amount due.		Yes; No; I'm not sure
Is there a written agreement, invoice or message about the underlying debt? (detailed round)
Why: Written proof of the debt strengthens the payee and is what the issuer will try to answer.		Yes; Only messages; No
Is either side open to settling? (detailed round)
Why: Cheque cases can be compounded (settled) at any stage. Courts encourage early settlement.		Yes; Maybe; No

3. Papers and steps listed

Paper / stepWhy / where / timeCorrection
The original cheque (some cases)The court will need to see the original. Where: Your bank returns it with the memo.
Bank return memo (cheque return slip) (some cases)Starts the 30-day clock and shows the reason. Where: Your bank, along with the returned cheque. Time: The demand notice is usually due within 30 days of receiving information of dishonour.
Bank statement showing the cheque deposit and its return (some cases)Shows the cheque was presented within its validity (generally 3 months from its date). Where: Net banking or branch.
Step: Send a written demand notice (through an advocate) and keep proof of dispatch (some cases)A complaint usually cannot proceed without it. Where: An advocate usually drafts it. Send by speed post / registered post (and email or WhatsApp if known). Time: Within 30 days of learning about the bounce.
Copy of the demand notice, postal receipts and delivery tracking (some cases)Delivery date starts the 15-day payment window and then the one-month window to file. Where: Your advocate, post office tracking.
Proof of the underlying debt (loan transfer, invoice, agreement, ledger) (some cases)The presumption of debt can be challenged; independent proof helps. Where: Your bank statements, invoices, agreements, account books.
Messages where the issuer admits the debt or promises payment (some cases)Admissions are strong evidence. Where: WhatsApp, SMS, email.
Issuer's full name, address, and account bank branch (some cases)Needed for notice and complaint. Where: Cheque, your records.
Company details and names of directors/partners who sign (some cases)Those in charge of the company may also be made accused. Where: MCA company search, letterhead, invoices.
The notice or summons you received, with its envelope (some cases)Paying within 15 of receiving the notice usually ends the matter. Where: Your post. Time: You usually have 15 days from receiving a demand notice to pay.
Proof of payments already made towards this debt (some cases)Reduces or disproves the liability. Where: Bank statements, receipts.
Records showing the cheque was given as security, or the deal it related to (some cases)Whether a "security" cheque can be the basis of a case depends on whether a debt existed when it was presented. Where: Agreement, loan papers, messages at the time.
Papers about the deal behind the cheque (agreement, invoices, loan papers, messages) (some cases)Your defence or settlement depends on what was actually owed. Where: Your records.
Step: A realistic payment or settlement proposal, with proof of what you can pay (some cases)Paying within the notice period, or settling later, can end the case. Where: Your bank statements and income records.
Your account statement around the cheque date (some cases)Shows balance, stop-payment instructions or closure. Where: Net banking.
Step: List of all cheques, dates, amounts and payments in the deal (some cases)Avoids confusion over amounts and multiple cases. Where: Your records.

4. Urgent warnings

LevelWarningCorrection
nowYour 30-day notice window is running — A written demand notice usually has to go within 30 days of learning about the bounce. See an advocate now; finish this list after.
nowThe filing window may be open now — If 15 days have passed since delivery without payment, the complaint is usually due within one month. Speak to an advocate this week.
soonA deadline may have passed — Late notices or complaints can sometimes be excused for good reason, and a civil recovery suit may still be possible. Ask an advocate.
nowA court case is already filed — Appear on the date in the summons, with an advocate. Ignoring a summons can lead to a warrant.
soonYou may have 15 days to respond — Paying within 15 days of receiving the notice usually prevents a case. If you dispute the debt, reply through an advocate.
