What the law says

Cheque dishonour is a criminal complaint under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act. Deadlines run from specific events, so exact dates matter. Since 1 November 2025 (Supreme Court, Sanjabij Tari), summons can be served by email and WhatsApp, the accused can pay online at the start, and settlement costs were reduced: no cost if paid before the accused's evidence, 5% before the trial court's judgment, 7.5% in the High Court and 10% in the Supreme Court.

Papers that usually help

  • The original cheque
    The court will need to see the original.
  • Bank return memo (cheque return slip)
    Starts the 30-day clock and shows the reason.
  • Bank statement showing the cheque deposit and its return
    Shows the cheque was presented within its validity (generally 3 months from its date).
  • Send a written demand notice (through an advocate) and keep proof of dispatch
    A complaint usually cannot proceed without it.
  • Copy of the demand notice, postal receipts and delivery tracking
    Delivery date starts the 15-day payment window and then the one-month window to file.
  • Proof of the underlying debt (loan transfer, invoice, agreement, ledger)
    The presumption of debt can be challenged; independent proof helps.
  • Messages where the issuer admits the debt or promises payment
    Admissions are strong evidence.
  • Issuer's full name, address, and account bank branch
    Needed for notice and complaint.
  • Company details and names of directors/partners who sign
    Those in charge of the company may also be made accused.
  • The notice or summons you received, with its envelope
    Paying within 15 of receiving the notice usually ends the matter.
  • Proof of payments already made towards this debt
    Reduces or disproves the liability.
  • Records showing the cheque was given as security, or the deal it related to
    Whether a "security" cheque can be the basis of a case depends on whether a debt existed when it was presented.
  • Papers about the deal behind the cheque (agreement, invoices, loan papers, messages)
    Your defence or settlement depends on what was actually owed.
  • A realistic payment or settlement proposal, with proof of what you can pay
    Paying within the notice period, or settling later, can end the case.
